How to use this note

**Don't memorize the answers word for word.** If you do, you'll sound like you're reading, and when you lose one word you lose the whole thing. Read the answer a couple of times to get the shape, then practice from the **Cue card** only. The cue card is what you actually remember in the room.

**Practice out loud.** Thinking the answer in your head doesn't count. Say it, record it on your phone, play it back. You'll hear right away where you ramble.

**Time it.** Most answers should run 60–90 seconds. "Tell me about yourself" can go up to 2 minutes. If you go longer, they stop listening and start waiting.

**Saturday practice (fits the 2-hour block in the Action Plan):** pick 3 questions, run each one out loud 3 times, and fill in one `TODO` with a real story.

**Where a `TODO` shows up,** I didn't have the real story in your notes. Don't make one up. A small true story beats a big invented one, because interviewers dig, and invented stories fall apart on the second follow-up.

# What every interviewer is really checking

Under almost every question, they're trying to answer four things:

1. **Can you do the job?** Skills, and proof you've used them recently.
2. **Do you actually want *this* job?** Or are you just applying everywhere?
3. **Will we like working with you?** Calm, honest, easy to talk to, no drama.
4. **Will you stick around?** Hiring is expensive. They're scared of someone who leaves in 6 months.

Before you answer, ask yourself which of the four this question is really about. That tells you what to stress.

# Two answer shapes

**For "about you" questions, use Now, Before, Next:**
- **Now:** what you do and your most recent work.
- **Before:** the one or two things in your history that matter for *this* job.
- **Next:** what you want, and why this role fits.

**For "tell me about a time" questions, use the story shape:**
- **The setup:** where you were, what was going on (one or two sentences, no more).
- **What I did:** the bulk of the answer. Say "I," not "we," for your part.
- **How it turned out:** a real result, even a small one.
- **What I took from it:** one sentence. This is the part most people skip, and it's the part that shows judgment.

# 1. Tell me about yourself

**What they're really asking:** "Give me the short version of why you're worth talking to for this job." It's also a test of whether you can summarize and communicate clearly, which matters a lot on remote teams.

**What a good answer does:**
- Stays under 2 minutes.
- Leads with recent, relevant work (Makpar), not 1993.
- Plants "hooks," meaning things you *want* them to ask about. Whatever you mention here, they'll follow up on, so you're setting the agenda for the rest of the interview.
- Ends by connecting to their role, which hands the conversation back.

**Your answer (about 90 seconds):**

> Sure. I'm a full-stack engineer, and I've been building software for about 25 years, mostly at small companies and startups where I've had to wear a lot of hats.
>
> Most recently I was at Makpar, where I led a team building a customer-facing portal for the U.S. Small Business Administration. The front end was React and Redux, hosted on S3 and CloudFront, and the back end was serverless: API Gateway, Python on Lambda, and DynamoDB. Since it was government work, testing and code quality really mattered, so we leaned hard on Jest, Playwright, ESLint, and SonarQube. We shipped it on time.
>
> Before that, I spent a lot of years on the PHP side, mostly Laravel, building eCommerce and CRM systems for small product teams. And earlier on I was CTO at a couple of telecom companies. The longest was Red Pocket Mobile, almost six years, where I ran IT, hired the team, and still wrote a lot of the code.
>
> What I'm looking for now is a small remote team where I can own things end to end, whether that's building features, making architecture calls, or helping the team get unstuck. That's what caught my eye about this role: `[one specific thing from their posting]`.

**Short version (30 seconds, for a recruiter screen):**

> I'm a full-stack engineer with about 25 years in, mostly at small companies. Most recently I led a team building a React and AWS serverless portal for the Small Business Administration. Before that, a lot of Laravel and eCommerce work, and some years as a CTO. I'm looking for a small remote team where I can own real problems, and this role looks like a good fit because `[reason]`.

**Cue card:**
- 25 years, small companies, lots of hats
- Makpar: led team, SBA portal, React + serverless, heavy testing, shipped on time
- Before: Laravel and eCommerce; CTO at Red Pocket for 6 years
- Want: small remote team, own it end to end
- Why them: `[one specific thing]`

**Watch out for:**
- **Don't walk through your whole résumé in order.** It's the most common mistake, and it buries Makpar.
- **Don't list languages.** "PHP, Python, React, Vue, Magento, Docker…" sounds scattered. Name the stack *inside* a story.
- **Don't mention the gap or the driving job here.** If they want it, they'll ask, and you have an answer ready (question 3).
- **The Army is optional.** Skip it by default. Bring it up for government, defense, or infrastructure roles, where "satellite communications, top secret clearance" is a strong hook.

**Likely follow-ups:** "Tell me more about the SBA portal" (question 5). "What was your role on that team?" (question 6). "Why are you looking?" (question 2).

# 8. What's your biggest weakness?

**What they're really asking:** "Are you self-aware, and are you honest?" A fake weakness ("I work too hard") is a red flag. They want a real one that won't sink the job, plus what you do about it.

**What a good answer does:** It names a real weakness, gives a quick example, and spends most of the time on what you do about it.

**Your answer (option A, breadth):**

> Honestly, I've spread myself wide. Twenty-five years at small companies means I've used a lot of different stacks, and for a while I'd describe myself by listing all of them, which made me sound like a generalist without a center. What I've done about it is pick a lane: React and TypeScript on the front, AWS serverless on the back, with Laravel when a traditional back end makes more sense. I still pick up new things fast, but that's where I go deep now.

**Your answer (option B, thinking time):**

> I do my best thinking when I've had a minute with a problem. I'm not the guy who blurts out the perfect answer in two seconds. What I've learned to do is ask a couple of clarifying questions first, write down what I'm hearing, and think out loud so people can follow along. It also means I catch a lot of things other people miss, because I'm not rushing.

**Cue card:**
- Real weakness: breadth (or thinking time)
- Example: used to list everything
- Fix: picked a lane, React + serverless, Laravel when needed

**Watch out for:**
- **Option B is honest and helps you in the room.** If you pause on a hard question later, they'll already know that's how you think. Just don't phrase it as "I'm bad at interviews." Phrase it as how you work.
- **Never pick a weakness that's the core of the job** (for example "I don't like writing tests" for a role that's all about quality).

# 16. Do you have any questions for us?

**What they're really asking:** "Are you curious, and are you actually evaluating us?" Always say yes. "No, I think you covered everything" reads as low interest.

**Ask 2 or 3 of these** (more in [[Prep Guide - 1]]):

1. "What's the biggest technical headache you're dealing with right now?" This is your best one. It gets them talking about real problems, and you can answer with "I dealt with something like that at…"
2. "What would a great first three months look like for this person?"
3. "How's the team set up? Who would I work with day to day?"
4. "What does the rest of the interview process look like?" Ask this early in the process. It helps you spot whiteboard or live-coding rounds ahead of time.

**Then close:**

> This sounds like a really good fit. `[One specific reason.]` I've done similar work at `[Makpar / Ronati / etc.]`, and I think I could be useful pretty quickly. What are the next steps?
